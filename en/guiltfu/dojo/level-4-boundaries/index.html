Level 4 — Boundaries

Belt green

GuiltFuDojoLearnDetectTrainDefendStories

The idea of this level

So far you have worked inward: the guilt, the voice, the rule. This level looks outward. Because guilt is not only manufactured inside your head: sometimes it arrives ready-made, in someone else’s sentence. “After everything I did for you.” “You’re making me suffer.” “If you really cared…” That is where the move is the block: the blow is coming, and you decide it does not land.

To block well, you need to tell apart what is actually reaching you. Not everything that influences you is manipulation. A rule is a rule. Someone trying to convince you with arguments is persuasion, and it is legitimate. Someone saying “I’d love it if you came” is influence, and it is normal between people who care about each other. Things change when the tool stops being the argument and becomes your emotion: when what is being used to move you is not a reason but your guilt. And they change again when there is no longer any real choice left: that is coercion.

This has to be said very clearly, because it is easy to walk out of this level seeing manipulators everywhere: recognizing manipulation is not accusing anyone. Most of the time it is not deliberate. Your mother does not get up in the morning planning how to use your guilt; she probably learned that sentence from her own mother, and it works without anyone ever having thought it through. Telling the mechanism apart is there to help you decide better, not to hand out blame in a different direction.

And a second thing, which begins here and will occupy the next levels: the fact that someone got upset does not mean you did something wrong. You can be responsible for your actions without being responsible for everyone else’s emotions. That border — guilt on one side, responsibility on the other — may be the most important one in this whole school.

The exercise

Look back over the past week and find one situation — just one — in which an emotion of yours (guilt almost always, but also fear or shame) was used, consciously or not, to influence a decision of yours. It can be big or tiny: anything from a plan you went along with reluctantly to a favor you agreed to with a forced smile.

Write it down in three steps:

  1. Attack. The sentence or the gesture, as literal as possible. Sometimes it is not a sentence: it is a silence, a sigh, a “no, it’s nothing”.
  2. Mechanism. Which emotion got switched on, and which rule it touched. (“I felt guilty because it lit up ‘a good son doesn’t leave his mother on her own’.”)
  3. Defense. What you could have done so the blow did not land, without escalating. Not a sharp reply: one that acknowledges the other person and still holds your decision. (“I understand it makes you sad, and I can’t this weekend. I’ll find another day.”)

Prepare the defense for next time, because there will be a next time: patterns repeat. The goal is not to win an argument; it is for that sentence to stop deciding for you.

The question to take with you

This level’s question

Did I really do something wrong, or did someone simply get upset?

When to move to the next level

When you can detect, in the moment or shortly after, that a sentence has tried to move you through guilt, and you can name the mechanism without turning the other person into a villain. You do not need to know how to say no yet; that is the blue belt. Mark the level when it feels that way — not when you finish reading.
